Warnings

[W1] GRADIENT_OPT/NUM_ITERS = 20 with GRADIENT_OPTIMIZER = adam is far below the
     floor. adam takes a normalised step of about INIT_VALUE_LR per iteration in
     the [-1, 1] scaled parameter space, so the floor is 1/INIT_VALUE_LR =
     1/1e-3 = 1000. At 20 iterations adam can travel about 20 * 1e-3 = 0.02 in a
     coordinate whose full range is 2 — one percent of one axis. The gradient
     stage would be very nearly a no-op.
     Remedy: raise NUM_ITERS to at least 1000, or switch to lbfgs, which is
     quasi-Newton and converges in tens of iterations. See R7 below: the
     evidence here favours keeping adam and raising the count.

Recommendations

[R1] Integrator family: implicit — the current INTEGRATOR = Kvaerno5 is correct
     Evidence: stiffness bounded by the Bendixson matrix measure over the box
          (autodiff J = df/dy at sampled states and parameters; no trajectory
          integrated):
              max |Re lambda|  <=  1.51e21 / s
          giving N_explicit = 5.59e4 s x 1.51e21 = 8.5e25 steps for stability,
          against MAX_STEPS = 100,000. An explicit method cannot complete a
          single solve anywhere near that corner.
          Smoothness: the RHS has exactly ONE discontinuity, the where() gating
          the second exotherm on T > T_ignite. It is one-way — T is
          non-decreasing, since dT/dt is a sum of absolute values and therefore
          >= 0 — so the trajectory crosses the threshold once and never returns
          through it.
     Why: R1 sends a stiff system with few one-way discontinuities to an
          implicit solver despite the non-smoothness. The Newton solve fails
          only at the single crossing, a bounded and survivable cost, whereas
          the stiffness would pin an explicit method's step over the entire
          5.59e4 s record. Kvaerno5 appears in lib/LLM/api/diffrax.md as
          implicit (stiff), is marked validated, and is high order — which R1
          prefers under severe stiffness, since the step is accuracy-limited and
          the per-step Newton cost is amortised over far fewer steps.
     Honest scope of this claim: the 8.5e25 figure is dominated by the extreme
          corner of the A1/A2 box, and those candidates are singular (see R3) —
          they would fail under any solver. The bound therefore establishes that
          the box ADMITS catastrophic stiffness, not that the physically
          relevant region is stiff. Note also that a diagonal-only reading is
          not sufficient here: the temperature row's off-diagonal entries carry
          factors of h1 and h2, which range to 1.2e3, so the coupling can exceed
          the diagonal by three decades and the full matrix measure is the
          appropriate tool.
     Edit: none — no change needed.

[R3] Bounds: narrow A1 and A2, currently 20 and 17 decades wide
     Evidence: A1 spans [1E5, 1E25] and A2 spans [1E8, 1E25] — 20 and 17 orders
          of magnitude. R3 flags anything beyond ~8 decades. The consequence is
          measurable rather than theoretical: the Bendixson bound above reaches
          1.5e21 /s, i.e. a characteristic time of 7e-22 s, at parameter sets
          whose reaction completes ~26 orders of magnitude faster than the
          5.59e4 s record. Such candidates are not slow, they are singular: the
          reaction goes to completion instantaneously and the temperature
          diverges in finite time.
     Why: a candidate in that region scores error_loss whatever the solver, so
          those decades are not being searched — they are being spent. With
          POPULATION_SIZE x NUM_ITERS = 18,000 evaluations over an 8-parameter
          box, decades that can only produce singular trajectories consume
          budget that never returns information. Narrowing on physical grounds —
          an Arrhenius pre-exponential for a condensed-phase decomposition is
          far from 1e25 — would concentrate the same budget on the reachable
          region. This is a judgement about the chemistry, so the exact bound is
          the user's call; the evidence only says the current upper decades
          cannot contain a solution.
     Edit: <P> MAX_VAL = ... </P> in the A1 and A2 blocks
           (MODEL/TRAINABLE_PARAMETER_DESCRIPTION), lowered to whatever the
           chemistry supports. Applying this is a full re-fit.

Applied

[R3] Bounds on the enthalpy scales: h1 MAX_VAL 1E3 -> 3.2E2, h2 1.2E3 -> 3.3E2.
    Evidence, from the data alone: the measured temperature rise is
        665 - 354 = 312 K. L3 already forces near-complete conversion
        (c1_end < 0.1, c2_end > 0.9), so the heat the model deposits is
        h1*dc1 + h2*dc2 ~ h1 + 0.96*h2, and that sum must equal the measured
        312 K. Neither enthalpy can therefore exceed ~312/0.96 individually
        without contradicting a directly measured quantity, whatever the other
        one does. The previous box allowed 1E3 and 1.2E3, and the resulting fit
        sat at h1 + 0.96*h2 = 1268 K -- 4.1x the measurement.
    Why this and not a loss term: an endpoint penalty was tried first and
        reverted (see below). Constraining the box is the better instrument
        here because the constraint is a bound, not a residual: it removes the
        overshoot from the search space instead of pricing it against other
        terms, so it cannot be traded away for a better rate match.
    Sweep evidence that enthalpy alone cannot be tuned post hoc: holding the
        fitted kinetics and scaling h1, h2 down by 30% does not lower the peak
        temperature, it EXTINGUISHES ignition -- T_end 1251 -> 368 K with
        c1_end 0.0001 -> 0.978, i.e. the reactant left unreacted, and L3 jumping
        to 130. In those rows h2 has no effect at all, confirming the second
        exotherm never fires. Reaction 1 self-heats, which raises k1
        exponentially, which produces more heat; below a critical h1 that
        feedback dies within the record and above it the system runs away, so
        scaling the enthalpies moves the system across an ignition bifurcation
        rather than sliding the peak temperature. The enthalpies and the
        kinetics are coupled through that threshold and must move together,
        which is why the box is narrowed and the whole fit re-run rather than
        the enthalpies adjusted.

[R8] Loss shape: an endpoint term was added to _compute_loss_problem and then
    REVERTED. The session ships the source's original loss. Recorded here
    because the experiment is informative and should not be repeated blind.
    Evidence, from the first completed fit: it scored L1+L2 = 0.2052 against the
        source's ~0.30 while ending at 1251 K where the data plateaus at 665 K.
        A better number on a worse answer. The measured total rise is
        665 - 354 = 311 K; the fit's was 897 K, ~2.9x too much heat.
    Why the existing terms cannot see it: L2 is an unweighted mean over 142
        samples of which ~91% lie in the flat pre-runaway phase, so the two or
        three points carrying the runaway barely register; and L3 bounds the
        endpoint only from below (T_end > 600), leaving the overshoot free. The
        model deposits h1*dc1 + h2*dc2 kelvin in total, so the enthalpy scales
        can inflate several-fold while the kinetics — and hence the six-decade
        heat-rate curve, L1 = 0.0743 — remain well matched. The rate-vs-temperature
        plot shows exactly this: an excellent fit over the measured range and an
        unconstrained tail beyond the last data point.
    Edit trialled: loss_endpoint = |T_sim[-1] - T_exp[-1]| / (max(T_exp) - min(T_exp)),
        added to the returned sum. It uses only the data's own final value, so
        it presumes no solution (cold_start.md).
    Outcome, and why it was reverted: the term did what it was designed to do
        and the fit still got worse. The endpoint came in at 638 K against the
        measured 665 K, and the enthalpies fell as intended (h1 889 -> 226,
        h2 394 -> 63), so the total heat budget balanced. But the optimum moved
        to a slow ramp: peak self-heating rate 0.043 K/s against a measured
        208.6 K/s, ignition ~3000 s early, and the log-rate term degraded from
        0.0743 to 0.2147. Pinning the endpoint constrains the total heat
        RELEASED; it does not constrain the SHARPNESS of the release, and the
        objective happily bought the former by selling the latter.
    The finding underneath, which no endpoint term can fix: L1 and L2 are both
        means over the DATA TIMES, and ~91% of the 142 samples lie in the flat
        pre-runaway phase. The runaway occupies two or three of them. Plotted as
        rate against temperature — the conventional ARC presentation — the
        measured points are spread evenly from 350 K to 665 K, which is where
        the information distinguishing a sharp runaway from a slow ramp
        actually lives. A loss evaluated at data times cannot see it. The
        principled fix is to evaluate the residual against temperature rather
        than time, interpolating the simulated rate onto the measured
        temperatures; that is a substantial modelling change and is left open.

    The 100,000 figure came from span/tau_min with tau_min taken from the
    thermal-feedback timescale — a STABILITY quantity, and therefore the wrong
    one for the implicit solver actually selected, whose step is
    accuracy-limited. The observed cost of the mistake was severe: with the

    cores, because candidates near the singular corners drive the Newton solve
    to retry repeatedly before failing. Both the XML and the literal in
    generated_script.py were changed, as jax_translation.md requires.

A1*exp(-Ea1/(kb*T)), so over the measured temperature range they are strongly
correlated and only their combination is likely to be identifiable. Expect that
pair to appear as a sloppy direction, and do not read either value alone as an
estimate. The same applies to A2 and Ea2.

================================================================================
WHAT THIS SESSION DEMONSTRATES ABOUT LOSS FORMULATION AND DATA DISTRIBUTION
================================================================================

This session is the clearest case in the repository of a fit being limited by
the objective rather than by the optimiser, the solver, or the search budget.
Three separate times a better loss number corresponded to a worse answer:

  1. The unbounded fit scored 0.2052 — better than the source's 0.30 — while
     overshooting to 1251 K and releasing 4.1x the measured heat.
  2. Adding an endpoint penalty fixed the overshoot and the optimiser paid for
     it by selling the rate match, because a penalty is a residual and residuals
     can be traded against each other. A BOUND could not be traded, and fixed
     the same problem cleanly. Where a quantity is measured and enters the model
     as a hard budget, constrain the box rather than adding a loss term.
  3. Tuning h2 and m2 by hand improved the transition-region rate error by 51%
     while making the configured loss twice as bad.

The root cause in every case is the same, and it is about where the data lives:

  **A residual averaged over sample points weights the fit by the DENSITY of
  those samples, not by where the information is.**

Here 91% of the 142 samples sit in the flat induction phase and only ~18% lie
above 480 K, so the runaway — the entire phenomenon the experiment exists to
measure — occupies a handful of terms in a 142-term mean. An objective built
that way cannot distinguish a sharp runaway from a slow ramp, and will trade
away decades of error in the transition to gain thousandths in the plateau.

The same data plotted against TEMPERATURE rather than time is distributed
evenly from 350 K to 665 K. That is why the conventional ARC presentation is
rate-vs-temperature, and it is the practical fix here: interpolate the
simulated rate onto the measured temperatures and take the residual there. The
data is not badly distributed in itself — it is badly distributed *with respect
to the independent variable the loss happens to use*.

Generalising, and worth carrying to other sessions:
  - Check how the samples are distributed over the feature the model must
    reproduce, not merely how many there are. Dense sampling in a region where
    nothing happens actively suppresses the region where everything happens.
  - Prefer the independent variable in which the information is spread evenly.
  - Prefer a bound to a penalty for anything directly measured.
  - A loss number is only comparable between two fits of the SAME objective.
    Reporting "we beat the published 0.30" meant nothing here until the
    trajectory was plotted.
